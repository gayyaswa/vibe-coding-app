Stock Portfolio Risk Analyzer

Vibe Coding Project  |  June 1–2, 2026  |  Stack: Python · Streamlit · Pandas · Plotly  |  Days completed: 2 of 3

1. Project Overview

Aspect Details
What I built A Streamlit web app that loads a stock portfolio from CSV, auto-assigns each holding to one of four risk buckets (Less Risk, Moderate, Growth, Aggressive), and helps the user rebalance their portfolio to match a target allocation. Day 2 added a visual-first portfolio overview (treemap), portfolio health scores vs. benchmarks, a blue/grey theme, and a full architectural refactor.
Problem solved Most portfolio trackers show what you own. This app tells you whether your current mix matches your risk appetite, how it compares to standard benchmarks (Conservative, Balanced, Aggressive Growth), and exactly what to buy or sell — in dollar amounts — to fix it.
Target user Individual retail investors holding a mix of ETFs, stocks, and bond funds who want a visual, actionable view of their risk profile.
Tech stack Python 3.9 · Streamlit (UI) · Pandas (data) · Plotly (charts) · Anthropic SDK (planned Day 3)
App sections Navigation: top tabs (changed from sidebar in Day 2)

1. Portfolio Overview — treemap (dominant), health score vs. 3 benchmarks, holdings detail (collapsible)
2. Allocation Dashboard — donut chart + target sliders with sum-to-100 validation
3. Rebalancing Engine — BUY / SELL / HOLD actions with dollar amounts
4. Sector Breakdown — grouped bar chart + sector × bucket heatmap
5. Gain/Loss Summary — P&L per ticker and per risk bucket
Architecture utils/ — Pipeline, Strategy (categorizer), Facade (PortfolioFacade), Factory (HealthScoreFactory), Registry (BENCHMARK_REGISTRY)
views/ — one render() function per tab section
app.py — ~60-line router (was 306 lines Day 1)
Tests 42 unit tests across 3 test files (test_categorizer.py · test_rebalancer.py · test_health.py). All pass.
Total portfolio value $110,139 market value · $80,960 cost basis · $29,179 unrealized gain (+36%)

2. Dataset Used

Sample data: data/portfolio_sample.csv — 20 synthetic holdings (5 per risk bucket) covering stocks, ETFs, and money market funds across 8 sectors. Prices are static (no live feed); this was a deliberate tradeoff to keep the app self-contained without API keys.

4. Iterations Tried

Day 1 — Foundation
Iteration What I Tried / Considered What Was Chosen Instead Why / How AI Collaboration Shaped This
Risk classification logic Sector-only rules (Technology → Growth for all tech stocks) Two-layer lookup: ticker override first, sector rule as fallback I knew MSTR was a Bitcoin proxy even though its sector label said Technology. The AI built the two-layer framework; I supplied the override values. This was my first clear lesson in how AI and human domain knowledge divide the work: the AI structures the problem, the human fills in the judgment calls the AI cannot make independently.
Slider sum constraint Auto-adjust sibling sliders so total always stays at 100% as user drags Independent sliders + real-time sum display + warning + disabled confirm button until sum = 100 I wanted auto-adjust — it felt like a better UX. The AI explained why it would break Streamlit's rerun model before writing a line of code. I didn't know this constraint existed. I asked why, understood the explanation, and then approved the alternative. Trusting the AI on framework-specific constraints I hadn't hit before was an early lesson in when to defer.
Rebalancing HOLD threshold Label every stock with a non-zero delta as BUY or SELL Deltas under $50 labeled HOLD I saw the raw output — "SELL $2.14 of MMF" — and immediately knew it was wrong in practice even if technically correct. I proposed the $50 threshold; the AI implemented it. This taught me that AI output needs human review not just for correctness but for usefulness. The code was right; the judgment about what makes output actionable was mine.
Delta distribution within buckets Sell the most overweight stock in a bucket first (concentrated approach) Distribute bucket delta proportionally across all stocks by current weight The AI presented both options neutrally and let me decide. I had to think about what kind of investor this app is for — passive vs. active. Proportional distribution matched the passive investor philosophy I was designing for. This was less about AI and more about me clarifying my own intent. Prompts that force a choice reveal assumptions you didn't know you had.

5. Learnings and Observations

Reflections on using Claude Code as an agentic AI tool to build this application — what shaped my thinking, what I'd do differently, what I now do by default.

Day 1 — Learning to use the tool
Category Observation
Plan before code — initial discomfort, then appreciation Entering Plan Mode before writing any code felt slow at first — I wanted to start building. But the architecture session paid for itself immediately. No wasted rework, no mid-implementation "wait, how does this connect to X?" moments. I now treat Plan Mode as a non-negotiable first step for anything touching more than a few files. The discomfort of pausing is a much smaller cost than the cost of reworking a wrong direction.
Tests as an afterthought that became a quality gate I asked for tests almost as an add-on ("can we also add unit tests?") without fully anticipating the impact. The tests caught a real bug — division by zero in the proportional weight calculation for empty buckets — that would have been a silent failure in production. I now understand why the convention in CLAUDE.md says "add tests for any new function in categorizer.py or rebalancer.py." It's not ceremony; it's a quality gate that pays back immediately.
Every prompt encodes a value judgment "Can we add unit tests?" was also "I want this code to be correct and verifiable." I didn't consciously frame it that way, but reflecting back, every prompt I wrote encoded a value: correctness, usability, maintainability, structure. The AI acts on stated intent — the values behind that intent come from me. Being deliberate about what I'm asking for is the same as being deliberate about what I'm building.
Domain knowledge is the irreplaceable human contribution The AI built the classification framework. I supplied the overrides — MSTR is a Bitcoin proxy, COIN is speculative finance, JNJ is a stable blue-chip despite being in Healthcare. None of these calls come from sector labels; they come from understanding what the assets actually are. The AI cannot make these calls independently. This is the part of the collaboration that cannot be delegated, and recognizing that early changed how I thought about my role in the process.
CLAUDE.md setup felt like overhead — until Day 2 At the end of Day 1, spending time on CLAUDE.md and agent memory felt like admin work. The return came at the very first message of Day 2 — I opened with "can you read the plan from claude.md?" and got back the full Day 2 scope, conventions, and constraints with zero re-explanation. The investment compounds: every convention I add to CLAUDE.md now saves explanation in every future session. I'd make this a Day 1 habit for any multi-session project.
Day 2 — Learning to direct the tool
Category Observation

I now treat "show me what this looks like" as a standard step before any visualization or layout decision. Building and then deciding you don't like it is far more expensive.

I caught this before any code was written and corrected it. Reviewing AI proposals critically before approving them is not a sign of distrust — it is the job of the human in the loop.

The AI mapped my mental model to what Streamlit can support rather than dismissing it. Cross-framework knowledge is an asset in AI-assisted development, not an obstacle.
